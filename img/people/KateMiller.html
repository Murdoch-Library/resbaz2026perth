    ✨ Understanding Restrictive Practices in the NDIS ✨ The NDIS Quality & Safeguards Commission has clear guidance on regulated restrictive practices — defined under the NDIS Act 2013 as any intervention that limits the rights or freedom of movement of a person with disability. Chemical | Environmental | Mechanical | Physical | Seclusion Because these practices can breach human rights, their use requires careful clinical and ethical consideration. They must always respect a person’s dignity and self-determination and be applied only within a PBS framework that prioritises proactive, person-centred, and evidence-based strategies. Restrictive practices should only be used in limited circumstances, as a last resort to prevent harm. ✅ Restrictive practices must only be used as a last resort and for the shortest time possible. ✅ They must be detailed in a behaviour support plan and authorised under state/territory processes. ✅ The focus is always on positive behaviour support, human rights, and dignity. ✅ Providers must report, review, and work towards reducing and eliminating restrictive practices. ✅ Culturally sensitive, trauma-informed approaches are essential to avoid re-traumatisation and uphold inclusion. ✅ Person-centred strategies: PBS focuses on understanding the individual, their needs, and the reasons behind behaviours of concern. ✅ Proactive approaches: Instead of reacting with restraint, PBS equips teams with tools to prevent escalation. ✅ Evidence-informed practice: Functional behaviour assessments guide tailored interventions that reduce reliance on restrictive practices. ✅ Collaboration & training: PBS builds staff confidence through coaching, reflective practice, and trauma-informed care. ✅ Quality of life first: By embedding meaningful activities and inclusion, PBS shifts the focus from “managing behaviour” to enhancing dignity and wellbeing.

    The National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research is the principal reference for research involving humans in Australia and is the guiding document for Human Research Ethics Committees.   The National Statement is released by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) and is periodically updated to stay relevant to evolving research practices, ethical understanding and community needs. The 2025 revision, originally due to take effect on 1 October 2025, has been postponed until early 2026. However, institutions are encouraged to adopt it earlier and Bellberry will apply the changes from 1 October 2025. The 2025 revision of the National Statement incorporates a fully revised Section 4 and consequential or minor changes to almost all other sections. The revised National Statement includes the following major thematic changes in Section 4: 🔹 framing the need for additional consideration for individual participants or groups of participants in terms of increased risk of harm, rather than in terms of vulnerability 🔹 emphasis on inclusion of individuals and groups frequently excluded or under-represented in research 🔹 drawing a distinction between research with people who are at increased risk due to characteristics or circumstances, and increased risk due to research contexts 🔹 removal of the need for research involving participants potentially at increased risk to automatically require review by a full HREC, enabling the use of alternative review processes for lower risk research with the same participants, where appropriate. The revisions also include recognition of issues related to research with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people and communities in the Preamble and Section 1 of the National Statement and a revised Chapter 4.7. We encourage all researchers to become familiar with the updates.     There’s a dangerous thread running through recent discussions about NDIS reform—one that dresses up old ideas in the language of economic “efficiency.” Sam Bennett’s article suggests that children with developmental delay and people with psychosocial disability are “poorly served” by the NDIS and would be better off with cheaper, standardised foundational supports. But beneath this logic is an uncomfortable truth: it echoes a mindset that once labelled people too costly, too complex, or unworthy of full support. This isn’t simply about redesigning a funding model. It’s about how we value human life, particularly the lives of those who don’t fit neatly into systems. When cost-efficiency rather than rights guides funding decisions, we return to a past where dependency was pathologised, diversity was medicalised, and some lives were deemed less worthy of investment. The idea that individualised supports should be replaced by “more efficient” group-based services revives the same ableist and eugenic assumptions that disabled people have spent decades resisting. What’s framed as reform risks becoming exclusion by stealth. The NDIS was built on principles of choice and control. Diluting these principles to chase budget savings undermines the very reason they exist. Instead, we must design systems that honour complexity, embrace cultural and linguistic diversity, and protect the right to flourish, not just survive. People with disabilities are not problems to be fixed or budgets to be trimmed. We are citizens, families, cultures, and communities—and we are worth supporting in full.

    If your NDIS plan review falls after mid-2026 this post is for you. The I-CAN assessment is coming. Here is how to prepare. The NDIS is moving to a new planning framework. Your budget will no longer be decided by a human planner looking at therapist reports. Instead a one to three hour interview will be fed into an algorithm to generate funding package based on your assessed support needs. The critical risk is this. Human delegates cannot manually override the computer-generated budget. The assessment captures who you are in that room on that day. It may not capture the hidden effort of masking, the exhaustion that builds across a week, or the difference between how you present in a quiet office versus real life. Internal NDIA documents reported by Crikey show the entire rollout is currently listed as red status. Every element is off track. The June timeline is at critical risk. That is not a reason to wait. It is a reason to prepare now. Because there is no override, your documentation must guide the assessor to input the right data the first time. Use this prompt to build your preparation "You are an NDIS planning expert familiar with the I-CAN framework. Context:  Age, diagnosis: [age, diagnosis, living situation] Day in the life: [what breaks down without supports] Current supports: [what is in place] Therapy reports: [key recommendations] My own voice: [what matters most to me] Masking and fluctuation: [how I present differently in clinical settings versus real life] Hidden effort: [what I manage internally that is invisible in a formal setting] Your task: 1.Map my support needs against the 12 I-CAN domains. Identify where my needs are likely to be underrepresented. 2.Identify environmental circumstances to flag in the mandatory questionnaire. 3.Flag support needs related to masking, fluctuating capacity, and hidden effort a structured interview may not capture. 4.Translate my needs into functional language a non-clinical NDIA assessor will understand. 5. Suggest three to five statements describing what I can do only when the right support is in place. 6. Identify gaps in my documentation that could weaken the assessment outcome. Format: Six numbered sections. Remove all identifying information." What to do if the report gets it wrong. If your assessment report does not reflect your support needs request a replacement assessment before final plan is approved. Once the plan is live you must go through the much harder internal review process. Do not wait. The broader concern. The Australian Psychological Society has raised alarms that I-CAN has not been validated for people with acquired brain injury or psychosocial disability. Your documentation is your only protection. Document everything now.     NDIS audits in 2026 are less about whether you have policies and more about whether you can produce credible, real-time evidence of what actually happened during service delivery. Auditors want to see that supports were delivered safely, properly, and in a way that connects to each participant’s goals-not just that invoices, policies, and training records exist. Your documentation (progress notes, case notes, support logs, rosters) must link together to tell the story of the service provided. The NDIS doesn’t force one specific note format, but it does expect records detailed enough to justify claims: who received support, what was delivered, when/where, quantity, and alignment with the service agreement and funded support item. For higher-risk or complex supports, additional evidence (including case notes) may be needed to show quality and appropriateness. Practically, providers use: Progress notes (after each shift): what happened, engagement, observable changes, goal alignment, immediate wellbeing concerns-used to verify billed hours match delivered supports and align with the plan. Case notes (when something significant occurs): behaviour/health changes, incidents/near misses, care plan updates, complex decisions, or family/allied health discussions-showing governance, risk management, and professional judgement. What auditors increasingly expect in 2026: Objective observations (avoid vague “went well” statements; include measurable detail). Clear goal alignment (document action → outcome → link to goals). Evidence that protocols were applied (e.g., dysphagia, epilepsy, behaviour support plans-policy must show up in practice). Participant voice (quotes or evidence of choice and control). Completing notes within 24 hours isn’t a legal rule, but it’s strongly recommended because late notes create inconsistency and weaken your position in audits or complaints. Common red flags include copy-paste notes, identical wording across participants, vague entries, and billing that doesn’t match documentation. Digital documentation reduces risk with required fields, timestamps, goal tagging, incident linking, and a clear audit trail-helping auditors verify evidence quickly. https://bit.ly/46WyETZ
